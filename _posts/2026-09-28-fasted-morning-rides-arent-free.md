A lot of the athletes I work with tell me some version of the same thing about early morning workouts: they can get away with it. An hour or ninety minutes on the bike or the trails with nothing beforehand, and they finish feeling fine. I don't doubt that. Most people can get through a session that length without fuel.

What I'm curious about is what "fine" is actually measuring. Finishing the workout is one outcome. How the rest of your day goes, and what the habit does for your race day, are two others, and those tend to get overlooked.

## The cost shows up in the hours after

Overnight your body keeps working, and it draws on the carbohydrate stored in your liver to keep blood sugar steady while you sleep. By morning those stores are lower than most people assume. Start a workout from there without adding anything, and the session runs on a partially drawn-down tank, followed by a day that still needs plenty of energy.

For most of the athletes I work with, that day is full: school, a full-time job, family, other responsibilities. A workout that felt fine on the bike can leave less in reserve for all of it, and the effect rarely gets connected back to a choice made at 5:30am. It's the same pattern as [fueling debt](/blog/fueling-debt/). A small gap doesn't announce itself, and it gets blamed on sleep, stress, or a busy afternoon.

## The rehearsal you're skipping

Here's a question I like to ask: are you going to do your race or your event fasted?

Most people plan to eat something before the start, which means the pre-race routine is something worth practicing. Early morning sessions are the most realistic place to do it. The gut adapts to what it's practiced with, just like your legs. Taking in a little fuel before you clip in or lace up gives your body repetition with having food in the system while you work, so race morning isn't a complete shock to the system. That repetition is something I've found to be essential for a lot of the athletes who find success in their events.

## "But isn't training fasted good for fat burning?"

This one comes up a lot. In most endurance efforts your body is using fat and carbohydrate at the same time, and the mix shifts with intensity. It isn't one or the other. At an easy aerobic effort, taking in some carbohydrate lets your body keep using its fat stores while making sure the carbohydrate stores don't get fully drawn down.

## What a "little something" can look like

None of this requires a full pre-race meal. The place it usually stalls is the athlete who isn't hungry in the morning. I hear it constantly: "I'm usually just not hungry in the morning. I just don't want to eat right away." That's a normal experience, and it's where small and simple works better than forcing down a meal.

A banana, a slice of toast with honey or jam, or a few chews or a gel all count. When solid food doesn't sound good, something liquid you can sip works too, like a carb drink mix or a recovery shake made and ready to go before you leave the house.

Something 15 to 30 minutes before heading out works well for most people. Some athletes notice a dip when they eat right around an hour out, so it's worth paying attention to how your own body responds. If a session is under an hour, you may not need anything during it, which puts even more weight on what happens before and after.

## A small change that made a big difference

One athlete I work with was spending more time in the gym and finding they had really low energy, even though the sessions were "only an hour." When we looked at their schedule, there was a window where they had no chance to fuel at all. We added a little fuel during the session, and I got a text that said, "To nobody's surprise, feeling a lot better after having a little something in my bottle or in my backpack to snack on during the gym."

It isn't a fasted morning ride, but it shows how little it can take.

## Try the comparison yourself

The most convincing evidence is usually your own. Over a week or two, pick two similar sessions. Do one the way you normally would, and do the other with a little something first. Notice how the session feels, how your energy holds up in the hour afterward, and how the afternoon goes. I like to call it me-search: you're using your own training as the test, and what you notice guides the next adjustment.

## The takeaway

Fasted morning sessions can feel free because the cost is delayed. You finish, you feel okay, and the bill arrives later in the day or on race morning. A small amount of fuel beforehand is one of the simplest ways to keep that from building, and it doubles as practice for the day it counts.
